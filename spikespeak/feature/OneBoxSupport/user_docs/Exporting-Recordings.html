Exporting recordings to SpikeGLX or Open Ephys#

SpikesPeak records its own .bin files with a JSON sidecar. Two converters turn a finished session into the formats the analysis ecosystem already reads:

cd python
python convert_to_openephys.py openephys  <recording-dir>  <output-dir>
python convert_to_spikeglx.py             <recording-dir>  <output-dir>

Both read the recording's .bin.json sidecars, so neither needs to be told the sample rate, the channel list, the probe or the map — those were written at record time from the hardware itself.

Keep the native .bin + .bin.json pairs. They are the archive. An export is a derived artifact you can regenerate at any time. The sidecar is the only place that carries the applied probe map, the probe serial, the basestation identity and the scale provenance, and no export format has a home for all of it.

What converts#

Band What it is SpikeGLX Open Ephys
raw Neuropixels 2.0 wideband, 384 ch ✅ ✅
adc OneBox analog input, 12 BNCs ✅ (obx) ✅
lfp Neuropixels 1.0 LFP band ✅ ✅
ap Neuropixels 1.0 AP band ❌ refused ❌ refused
nidaq NI-DAQ analog input ✅ ✅
nidaq_di NI-DAQ digital lines ✅¹ ✅
adc_di OneBox comparator logic band ❌ no such stream ✅

¹ nidaq and nidaq_di cannot share one SpikeGLX run, and the float64 → int16 conversion is opt-in and irreversible. Prefer Open Ephys when you need both.

A 2.0 session on a OneBox — the usual case — records raw + adc, and both convert losslessly to both formats. The int16 counts are copied byte for byte; nothing is rescaled on the way out.

Why the NP1.0 AP band is refused#

Not a converter limitation — a property of the recording. The AP band's per-frame timestamp is not a usable per-sample clock: inside each 12-sample IMEC packet the stamps advance about 4.36 ticks per sample and then step back by 8, netting exactly 40 ticks per packet. Roughly 8 % of frames therefore carry a timestamp that does not advance on the previous one.

Forcing those to increment would stretch the reconstructed time axis to about 1.31× the span the timestamps imply and invent six figures' worth of gaps in a recording that may have lost nothing. Both tools refuse rather than write a plausible-looking lie.

The AP samples themselves are fine. What is missing is a per-sample time base. Convert the lfp band instead (--band lfp), which is exactly uniform.

What is not a gap#

data.lfp and data.spike — the derived filter bands — cannot be recorded at all, by design. They are the raw band plus documented coefficients, so they are reproduced offline exactly rather than written to disk twice. Nothing is missing from your export because they were never in it.

Before an experiment#

Do a 30-second dry run and convert it both ways. It takes two minutes and turns every item below from a post-hoc discovery into a pre-flight check.

Then look at three things in the raw sidecar (<name>_raw_<stamp>.bin.json):

  1. num_channels is 384, not 3. The profile and the GUI channel selection both silently narrow a recording, with no warning. A 3-channel in-vivo recording cannot be recovered.
  2. metadata_version is 2 and scale is not null. A version-1 sidecar has no scale block, no frame block, no columns and no acquisition block — you lose the applied map, the probe serial and the basestation identity.
  3. scale.gain reads 100 on a commercial 2.0 probe (NP2003/4/13/14). Prototypes read 80.

After each recording, before moving anything:

python convert_to_openephys.py scan <recording-dir>

It reports frames and gaps per band. A dropped-sample gap found now can still be acted on.

Things that will surprise you#

The ripple sidecar is written beside the recording folder, not inside it. The bands go into <dir>/<name>/, but <name>_ripple.json lands in <dir>/. Archive the parent directory, or copying "the recording folder" leaves behind the detection channels and trained thresholds.

Per-event detections and stimulation times are not written to disk by the backend. They exist as live WebSocket messages. If you need a per-event record of a closed-loop session, capture the WebSocket stream yourself while it runs — no converter can recover it afterwards.

Do not rename output_filename in a profile. The sidecar's band name comes straight from it and both converters key their tables off that string. A renamed band stops converting.

The OneBox ADC's declared rate is the profile's number. Profiles say 30300 Hz; the device runs about 30300.5. That is ~16.5 ppm — roughly 60 ms over an hour — and only matters if you time by sample index rather than by the recorded tick counter.

Which format should I use?#

Open Ephys, unless you specifically need SpikeGLX. It accepts every band a OneBox produces (including the comparator logic band), records gaps honestly in sample_numbers.npy rather than implying a contiguous file, and its structure.oebin carries per-channel shank, xpos and ypos derived from the applied map.

SpikeGLX is the right choice when your pipeline is built on CatGT / TPrime, or when you want the .meta to be the familiar artifact. The exported ~imroTbl is the applied map verbatim, and ~snsGeomMap carries real per-channel shank — so a four-shank map exports as four shanks.

Neither export has been loaded by the vendors' own readers as part of a test. The unit tests are self-checks. The native files remain the archive; treat an export as something to verify the first time you feed it to a new tool.
