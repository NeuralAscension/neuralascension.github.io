SpikesPeak — Spike Audio Monitor (User Guide)#

Listen to one channel through the speakers while you stream. The signal is band-passed to the spike band, everything small is silenced, and what survives is played — so single units come out as discrete clicks rather than continuous hiss. It is the fastest way to tell a good electrode from a dead one without staring at a trace.

New here? GUI-Guide.md covers launching SpikesPeak and connecting a source; SimulatedData.md covers the hardware-free sources you can practise on. The one simulated scenario that has the audio monitor is Simulated Data… ▸ Neuropixels 2.0 Multishank — sine wave.

For developers: the DSP, the thread model and the device-selection logic are in ../developer_docs/SpikeAudio.md; the measured audio/video latency work is in ../developer_docs/AudioVideoLatency.md. This guide does not duplicate them — it is the operation manual.

The 30-second version: press ♪ in the toolbar → click a channel label in the Live Data view → raise Volume → set Thresh until the hiss stops and the clicks do not → check the device button says something other than MME.

1. Open the monitor#

  1. Connect a source and open the Live Data tab.
  2. Click the ♪ button in the toolbar's second row (to the left of ◉ Ripple Detection). The ♪ Audio dock opens directly underneath the button, the same way the Bands menu does.

📷 the ♪ button and the dock opening under it

  1. Drag the dock by its ♪ Audio header if it covers a trace you want to watch. × closes it.

What you should see: a six-row panel — the channel row, Thresh / Volume / Boost sliders, an in / out level meter, and a footer with the output-device button and Mute. Under it, one grey line naming the band it is listening to.

The ♪ button only appears once a source that has audio is connected, and today that means a Neuropixels 2.0 source. These are the profiles that ship with the monitor:

Every probe source has it — 1.0 and 2.0 alike. What decides it is whether the source streams a probe band, not which profile you picked: if data.npx.ap or data.npx.raw is present and the profile did not declare a monitor, the backend creates one anyway and logs Auto-provisioned proc.audio (the profile did not declare one).

That is deliberate. Only the 2.0 profiles name proc.audio in their YAML, which used to mean a 1.0 rig had no spike audio at all — not because 1.0 cannot do it (it streams a real AP band, which is exactly what a spike monitor wants) but because nobody had added the line.

An NI-DAQ-only source has no ♪ button, because it streams no probe band. That is the one case where the button is legitimately absent.

2. Choose a channel — this is the step people get wrong#

The dock does not pick a channel for you, and it will tell you so. Which channel is worth hearing is a judgement you make by looking at traces, so you make it in the traces:

  1. Leave the audio dock open.
  2. Click a channel LABEL in the Live Data view — the little tag at the left of a trace (14L, 196, …), not the trace itself.

📷 clicking a channel label with the dock open

What you should see: the label turns green and glows, and the dock's top row changes from a grey italic nothing selected / click a channel label to the channel's name in green. Sound starts immediately.

  • Clear stops listening without muting. The row goes back to nothing selected.
  • Click a different label to move the monitor. Only one channel is monitored at a time; it is fed to both speakers (mono).

⚠️ Clicks only reach the monitor while the dock is open. With the dock closed, clicking a label does what it always did — toggles that channel's zoom selection, so the scroll wheel over the chart zooms it alone. With the dock open, a label click only goes to the audio monitor, so close the dock before selecting traces to zoom or you will change what you are listening to instead.

If both the audio dock and the ripple dock are open, audio wins the click.

Neither of these is the recording selection — that lives in Probe Map ▸ Select Recording Channels, and nothing you click in the live view changes it.

If you hear nothing and the row still says nothing selected, that is the whole problem. The backend says so too — the console prints:

[AudioProcessor] monitor is ON but NO CHANNEL IS SELECTED -- <what just happened> -- it will be
silent. Open the audio dock, then click a channel LABEL in the live view (the clicks only reach the
monitor while the dock is open).

3. Thresh, Volume, Boost#

All three take effect as you drag — finding a threshold is a listening task, so you hear the change under your finger rather than after you let go.

Slider Range Default What it does
Thresh 0 – 400 ADC counts 25 A digital diode: anything within ± that many counts is silenced outright, and what is left is shifted toward zero. This is what turns a hissy band into discrete clicks.
Volume 0 – 100 % 50 % Plain loudness.
Boost ×1 – ×1000 ×1 Extra gain on top of Volume, log-scaled across the slider. ×1 changes nothing.

Setting Thresh#

  1. Start at 0 — you will hear the whole spike band, hiss included.
  2. Raise it until the background hiss disappears.
  3. Keep raising only until the clicks start disappearing too, then come back down a little.

A spike on a Neuropixels probe is tens to low hundreds of counts, which is why the slider stops at 400 — a wider slider would spend most of its travel in silence.

When to reach for Boost#

Only when Volume at 100 % is still too quiet — a quiet preparation, a high-impedance site, a laptop speaker. Turn Volume up first; Boost is the second stage, not the first. It is log-scaled because a linear ×1–×1000 control would put every useful setting in its first millimetre.

Boost amplifies whatever survived the threshold, including the noise floor. If ×100 gives you loud hiss rather than loud spikes, the answer is a higher Thresh, not more Boost.

Mute#

Mute (footer, right) silences the monitor but keeps your channel selected — it turns red and reads Unmute. That is different from Clear, which forgets the channel.

On a Neuropixels 2.0 probe, un-muting also switches the Spike band on in the Bands menu, so what you are hearing is also on screen; muting switches it back off. If you turned the Spike band on yourself, it stays on — the monitor only puts back what it took.

4. The in / out meter — read this before you debug anything#

Two bars, updating four times a second while the dock is open. They exist to answer one question: is there no signal, or is there signal that something is silencing?

Bar What it measures
in The channel after filtering, before Thresh and Volume — peak in ADC counts. "Is there anything in the spike band at all?"
out What actually reaches the speaker, after Thresh, Volume and Boost — peak out of 32768.

Both are log-scaled (the bottom of the bar is −60 dBFS) because the signals worth hearing are often a few counts out of 32768 and would be invisible on a linear meter. A bar turns amber near full scale — that is your clipping warning: back off Volume or Boost.

The diagnosis table#

in out What it means What to do
moving moving Working. Nothing.
moving flat The threshold is eating the signal. There is spike-band activity on this channel; all of it is inside the diode's dead zone. Lower Thresh. If out moves but is too quiet, raise Volume, then Boost.
flat flat The problem is upstream of the audio. Nothing is arriving on that channel. Check you are streaming (▶), that a channel is actually selected, that you are not muted, and that the site is not dead or disconnected. Click a neighbouring label and see whether that one moves.
flat moving Not possible. —

Mute and "no channel selected" flatten BOTH bars, not just out. So before you use the meter as a diagnostic: pick a channel, and make sure the footer button reads Mute (not Unmute).

5. Choose the output device — it matters far more than it looks#

This is not a convenience control. It is a latency control worth up to 90 ms, and it is the single largest audio-latency win available anywhere in SpikesPeak.

Windows enumerates the same physical speakers once per audio protocol (MME, DirectSound, WASAPI, WDM-KS). This rig reports fourteen output devices for three real outputs. Same speakers, wildly different delay.

How to pick one#

  1. Click the device button in the footer — it shows what is playing right now.
  2. The menu groups by speaker, not by protocol: one heading per physical output, then one row per protocol underneath it.
  3. A lit green dot marks the fastest route to that speaker. Pick a lit dot.

📷 the device menu, grouped by speaker, with green dots on the fastest routes

  1. System default (top of the menu, always lit) follows your Windows output setting and reaches it by the fastest available protocol — change the output device in Windows and the sound moves with it, with no gap and nothing to click here.

What you should see: the button repaints with the device that was actually opened. If it snaps back instead, that device refused the format and the backend kept the old one — pick another route. The panel never shows you a selection that is producing no sound.

The measured numbers, on this rig, 2026-08-25#

raw → ear is the whole delay, from a probe sample to the speaker. Rows are ordered by measured output latency — which is what the green dot ranks on — so the raw → ear column is deliberately not in order: it also carries each device's ring depth, and that moves independently.

Route Measured output latency raw → ear
DirectSound → Primary Sound Driver (what System default takes) 8.7 ms ~37 ms
DirectSound → ASUS VE278 — the monitor speakers 13.1 ms ~47 ms
DirectSound → LG HDR WFHD 13.1 ms ~48 ms
DirectSound → Digital Audio (S/PDIF) 18.1 ms ~49 ms
WASAPI → Speakers (NoMachine — a virtual device) 20.0 ms ~30 ms
WDM-KS → SPDIF Out (digital; may not reach a speaker at all) 19.9 ms ~23 ms
MME → Sound Mapper 99.9 ms ~116 ms

What to act on:

  • Prefer a lit dot. That is the whole rule, and it is why the dot exists.
  • Avoid MME. It measured 99.9 ms against DirectSound's 8.7 ms on the identical output — five to ten times worse than anything else, and the one finding here solid enough to state as a rule.
  • "Use WASAPI" is wrong on this machine. It is the usual advice, and here WASAPI refuses to open on every one of this rig's three real outputs — the actual monitor speakers included — and succeeds only on a virtual NoMachine device. WDM-KS refuses on two. The advice that survived measurement is DirectSound.
  • Ignore any latency figure a driver advertises. DirectSound promises the worst number in the table (120 ms) and delivers the best. The dot is ranked on what was measured, not what was promised.
  • The System default button does not name the endpoint it chose. The backend console does:

[AudioWorker] Audio output device: Primary Sound Driver via Windows DirectSound (follows the system default)

If the speakers go away mid-session#

Unplug them, disable them, or let another program take them exclusively, and the monitor would otherwise just go quiet for the rest of the session with no error anywhere. It does not: a watchdog checks the stream once a second and, when there is no working device, re-scans the machine and reopens on whatever is available now. The same path picks up speakers you plug in after starting, so you never have to restart to use them.

The re-scan itself is backed off to one attempt every five seconds — re-enumerating is not free, and on a rig with no audio at all this branch would otherwise run forever. So allow up to about five seconds for a device to be picked up, not one.

6. What you are hearing, and how late#

Path Delay from the probe sample
eye — the trace on screen ~19 ms (the live view publishes at ~50 Hz)
ear — sound from the speaker ~47 ms at best on real speakers via DirectSound; ~116 ms if it lands on MME

You always see it before you hear it. The gap is about 28 ms on a good device and about 97 ms on MME — four to five display frames.

The audio is NOT a timing reference.#

It is for hearing spike character — rate, shape, whether a site is alive, whether you are in the cell layer. Nothing that depends on when something happened may be taken from it: not latency, not alignment to a stimulus, not "the click came before the flash". Anything that needs timing must come off the recording, where every sample carries its own timestamp.

Almost none of that delay is SpikesPeak's — it is the Windows audio stack and the speaker's own buffering. The figures come out the same on a simulated source as on a real probe, which is exactly how we know it is not the probe's. Full method and per-stage breakdown: ../developer_docs/AudioVideoLatency.md.

7. Which band it listens to#

The dock's grey footer line tells you, per source. There is nothing to configure — it is decided from what the probe actually streams:

Probe Band it listens to The line in the dock
Neuropixels 1.0 The probe's own AP band, passed through unfiltered. The probe has already banded it; that is the spike band. AP band · straight from the probe
Neuropixels 2.0 2.0 has no AP band, so the 300 – 6000 Hz spike band is derived inside the audio processor. AP / spike · derived 300-6000 Hz

Both are correct, and both sound like a spike monitor. The distinction is shown because "AP / spike" alone would not tell you which of the two you are hearing — and on 1.0 it means the monitor is doing no filtering at all.

You will see whichever line matches the probe you are on. Both are reachable: the monitor is auto-provisioned on any probe source (§1), so the 1.0 row is live behaviour, not a hypothetical.

8. Troubleshooting#

No sound at all#

Work down this list in order; the first two account for most cases.

  1. No channel selected. The channel row reads nothing selected. → Open the dock, then click a channel label in the Live Data view (§2). Both meter bars flat is the tell.
  2. Not streaming. Audio comes from live samples. → Press ▶.
  3. Threshold above the signal. in moves, out is flat. → Lower Thresh (§4).
  4. Muted. The footer button reads Unmute in red. → Click it.
  5. Too quiet. out moves but you hear nothing. → Raise Volume, then Boost (§3). Check the Windows volume mixer too.
  6. Wrong device. The sound is going to a monitor's HDMI output, or to an S/PDIF socket with nothing plugged into it. → Click the device button and pick the speakers you are actually sitting in front of (§5).
  7. The device refused to open. You picked one and the button snapped back to the previous device. → Pick a different protocol for the same speakers — a lit dot.

"No audio output device was found"#

The panel greys its controls out and shows an amber banner:

No audio output device was found, so nothing will be heard. Acquisition and recording are unaffected.

That second sentence is the important one, and it is literally true: a machine with no speakers still connects probes, streams and records normally. The audio monitor is a convenience laid over acquisition, not a part of it. The reason is in the backend console:

[AudioProcessor] No audio output available -- <reason> -- the audio monitor is disabled for this
session. Acquisition is unaffected.

Plug speakers in and the watchdog picks them up within about five seconds (§5) — no restart needed.

The ♪ button is not in the toolbar#

No source is connected, or the connected source is not one of the Neuropixels 2.0 profiles that carry the monitor. It does not appear on an NI-DAQ-only source, which streams no probe band. See the table in §1 for the profiles that have it.

Clicking a channel label zooms the trace instead of switching the sound#

The dock is closed. Open it first — label clicks only reach the monitor while it is open (§2).

It sounds like loud hiss, not clicks#

Thresh is too low, or Boost is doing work Thresh should be doing. Raise Thresh until the background disappears, then set the level with Volume and Boost.
