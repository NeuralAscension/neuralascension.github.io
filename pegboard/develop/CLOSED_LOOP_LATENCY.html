This is not the number in the paper, and should not be

The published description of this platform — Dutta, Ellett and Welle, IEEE NER 2025, pp. 119–124 — reports 212.5 ms predicted and 213–215 ms measured. This report's headline is larger. That is a difference of where the interval starts, not of speed, and the two figures should never be quoted against each other.

Like for like, the detection chain is as fast as published. The paper's 212.5 ms measures the same interval this report calls Board total: 211.07 ms here, measured on the board at microsecond resolution over 50 placements. The chain did not get slower. Eight-sample averaging costs about 68 ms more than the paper's two reads, and a shorter settle plus a faster confirming read return it.

The headline is larger because it adds the 94 ms sampling lag in front: the peg lands between two converter reads, and the board cannot know about it until the next one completes. The paper's chain does not count that interval, so its figure describes the firmware rather than the participant's experience.

Quote the headline, not the board total. The participant waits from the moment the peg lands, and the sampling lag is part of that wait however it is accounted for. The board total is the right number only when the question is specifically about firmware cost, or when comparing against the paper. Which of the two a figure refers to should always be stated.

Other differences between the published build and this one — transport, thresholds, the settle, the board variant — are set out in Updates since the paper.

Method

Where the interval starts

At the peg touching down — not at the firmware noticing. The landing instant is reconstructed from samples.csv: for each stimulation edge, the load trace is walked back to the first sample materially above the preceding baseline. The peg landed somewhere between that sample and the one before it, and the gap between them is reported as the sampling lag rather than hidden.

Where it ends

At the rising edge on Pi GPIO26, timestamped by the kernel against CLOCK_MONOTONIC at interrupt time — the same clock t_ms is defined on. This is the most trustworthy instant in the recording: no model sits between the event and its timestamp, and it is the same wire the stimulator sees. Nothing here relies on the firmware's own account of when it fired.

Why two views

The outside view owes nothing to the firmware but cannot see inside its ~200 ms. The board's stage timings resolve that interval to microseconds but cannot see the sampling lag, because the board does not know when the peg landed either. They overlap on the firmware interval, and disagreement there would mean one of them is wrong.

What the instrumentation costs

Four micros() calls in the detection path, about 2 µs in total. The trigger timestamp is taken immediately after TRIG_HIGH(), never before, so nothing is inserted into the interval the participant waits on. The profile_cost self test measures this on the board and fails above 1 ms.

What is not reducible

The sampling lag is bounded by the converter's period and can only shrink by sampling faster. The ~200 ms of averaging and re-checking is not overhead: it is what months of testing across MS patients and healthy controls established as necessary to separate a genuine placement from a knock to the table. It is documented here, not argued with.

python3 analysis/latency_report.py \
